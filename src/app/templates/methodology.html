Methodology

How the trend score is calculated.

Slopes

For each stock we fit a straight line to the log of its daily adjusted closing price over a trailing window (5 years, 1 year, 1 month). The slope is reported as the % change along that trend line over its window, so a 1Y slope of +20% means the trend line rose 20% over the past year. Log prices make slopes comparable between a $5 stock and a $500 stock, and a regression is less sensitive to a single spike than a simple start-to-end return.

Trend score

Slopes over different windows aren't on the same scale (a 5-year move is naturally much larger than a 1-month one), so blending raw slopes would let one window dominate. Instead each slope is converted to a percentile rank across all stocks (0–100), and the trend score is the weighted average of those percentiles:

Stocks with less than five years of history are scored on the slopes they do have. A stock needs at least one year of history to be ranked. Tickers with no recent prices, or with a single-day move of 5x or more (usually an unadjusted split or bad data), are dropped. You can try your own weights with Tune weights on the rankings page.

Trend score vs. the prediction model

The trend score is calculated from slopes only. There's no model behind it, and it describes past momentum rather than predicting future prices. A score of 90 means a stock has been trending better than about 90% of stocks, not that it will keep doing so.

The Portfolio Sandbox uses a separate gradient-boosted tree model to forecast each stock's price 21 trading days (about 30 calendar days) out. It reuses the same slope calculations as inputs, over more windows (1 year, 6 months, 3 months, 1 month, 2 weeks and 3 days), and adds volatility, drawdown, beta, sector and industry-momentum, and market-wide (VIX, recent market return) features. It's trained on hundreds of past dates per stock to predict how each stock does relative to the market. Two quantile models give each forecast a 10th–90th percentile range, and the sandbox combines those ranges in a Monte Carlo simulation to project a whole portfolio.

The model is checked walk-forward: each of the last four 3-month blocks is predicted by a model trained only on dates before it, and a feature is kept only if it improves the ranking of stocks in most blocks (moving averages, trading volume and earnings timing were tried and didn't). Short-term returns are mostly noise, so it only narrowly beats simply assuming no change; the sandbox's How it works panel shows the latest numbers.

Sector and industry come from Yahoo Finance's current classification. The ticker list dates from 2017 and companies that have since been delisted have no price history, so the model only ever learns from survivors, which flatters its historical numbers somewhat.

Data

Prices come from Yahoo Finance via yfinance and are refreshed by re-running the modeling pipeline. Nothing here is investment advice.
